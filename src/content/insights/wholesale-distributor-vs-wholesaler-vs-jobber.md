A wholesaler, a wholesale distributor and a jobber all buy goods and resell them to other businesses rather than to consumers. The U.S. Census Bureau's industry classification actually groups them together as merchant wholesalers: businesses that sell goods on their own account. In day-to-day trade, the words tend to signal different things about scale, sourcing and service, and those differences show up in your minimums, your pricing and your paperwork.

So the useful question isn't which label a supplier uses. It's how that supplier actually operates. This guide breaks down what each term usually means, where they blur, and what to ask so you know what you're buying from.

## The short definitions

- **Wholesaler:** The broadest term. Any business that buys goods in volume and resells them to retailers, other wholesalers or commercial users. It says little about how the business sources or services customers.
- **Wholesale distributor:** A wholesaler that typically holds inventory, handles logistics and maintains ongoing supply relationships, often with brands directly or through their authorized networks. The word "distributor" usually implies a continuing service relationship, not one-off sales.
- **Jobber:** A middleman who buys from manufacturers or large distributors and resells to smaller retailers, often in smaller quantities, mixed assortments or a particular territory. The term is older and is still common in consumer goods, auto parts and some regional trades.
- **Rack jobber:** A specialized jobber that stocks and maintains its own displays inside a retailer's store, sometimes on consignment. It is a different model from buying cases outright.
- **Agent or broker:** Arranges sales between buyers and sellers for a fee or commission, but doesn't take ownership of the goods. The Census Bureau classifies agents and brokers separately from merchant wholesalers for exactly this reason.

## What the official classification says

The North American Industry Classification System (NAICS), which federal agencies use to categorize businesses, puts wholesale trade in Sector 42. Within it, the Census Bureau lists wholesale merchants, distributors and jobbers together as businesses that sell goods on their own account, and notes that they typically maintain their own warehouses where they receive and handle goods.

Agents and brokers sit in their own subsector (NAICS 425). The defining feature is that they don't take title to the goods. They connect a buyer and seller and earn a fee or commission.

> **Note:** No one hands out a license for calling yourself a "distributor" instead of a "wholesaler." Some industries do have licensing, such as alcohol, tobacco and prescription drugs, but for general consumer goods the label is a business choice. Treat it as a claim to check, not a credential.

The practical takeaway: the most important line isn't between distributor, wholesaler and jobber. It's between a supplier that **owns the inventory it sells you** and one that **arranges a sale of someone else's inventory**. That affects who invoices you, who stands behind a shortage or a damaged case, and what your paper trail looks like.

## How they compare in practice

The table below reflects how the terms are commonly used in consumer goods. Individual businesses vary, so use it as a starting point for your questions.

| Term | Wholesale distributor | Wholesaler (general) | Jobber | Broker / agent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Owns the goods it sells** | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |
| **Typical sources** | Brands, authorized distributors, other established channels | Varies widely | Manufacturers and larger distributors | Represents a seller or buyer |
| **Typical buyers** | Retailers, chains, ecommerce sellers, other wholesalers | Retailers and other businesses | Smaller and independent retailers | Buyers matched to a seller's goods |
| **Order unit** | Cases and pallets, often mixed across brands | Cases, pallets or truckloads | Smaller or mixed quantities | Often full lots or truckloads |
| **Ongoing service** | Usually an account with a named contact | Depends on the business | Often route- or territory-based | Per deal |
| **Who invoices you** | The distributor | The wholesaler | The jobber | Usually the seller, sometimes the broker |

### Where the terms blur

Plenty of businesses fit more than one column. A distributor that serves small independent stores in one region is functionally a jobber. A wholesaler that places recurring orders with brands and runs a warehouse is, in practice, a distributor. Some companies describe themselves as "distributor and jobber" precisely because they do both: they hold stock for regular accounts and also buy opportunistically, for example closeouts or excess inventory, to resell to smaller buyers.

That's not a red flag. What matters is that the supplier is clear about which model applies to the goods you're buying.

## Why the difference matters to a buyer

### Pricing and minimums

Suppliers higher up the chain, closer to the brand, often have better unit costs but higher minimums, such as full pallets or truckloads, and they may only sell to buyers above a certain size. Jobbers and smaller distributors add a margin, but they'll usually sell by the case and let you mix brands. For many retailers and online sellers, paying slightly more per unit is worth it to avoid tying up cash in a pallet of one SKU.

### Sourcing and authenticity

A distributor buying directly from a brand or its authorized network can usually give you a clean chain of invoices. A jobber may be buying from several upstream sources, including the secondary market, where branded goods are bought outside the brand's official channel. Those goods can be genuine and legal to resell, but some brands and marketplaces treat them differently. If you sell on Amazon or Walmart, ask about sourcing before you order, not after a listing gets flagged.

### Service and accountability

When a broker arranges a deal, the seller ships the goods and handles problems, and the broker may or may not help. When you buy from a distributor or jobber that owns the stock, they're responsible for what they ship. That matters the first time an order arrives short, damaged or with the wrong lot dates.

### Assortment

Distributors that specialize in a few categories tend to go deeper in each one. Someone buying [household essentials](/categories/household/) wants every common size of the products that move, not a scattering of closeout items. Food and grocery has its own pressures, such as dates, rotation and storage, so ask how a supplier handles date codes in [food & grocery](/categories/food-grocery/) before you commit.

## Questions to ask any supplier, whatever it calls itself

- Do you own the inventory you're selling me, or are you arranging the sale?
- Who will the invoice come from, and what will it show?
- Where do you buy the brands I'm ordering: from the brand, an authorized distributor or other sources?
- What's your order minimum, and can I mix brands and SKUs?
- How do you handle shortages, damage and short-dated product?
- Is this regular stock you'll carry again, or a one-time lot?

The answers tell you far more than the label on the website.
