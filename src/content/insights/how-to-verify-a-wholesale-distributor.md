To verify a wholesale distributor, confirm four things before you pay: the business legally exists under the name on its invoices, it operates from a real address you can check, the people you're dealing with actually work there, and the payment details belong to that business. Then confirm it can document where its goods come from, and place a small test order before a large one. None of this takes long, and it catches the problems that are expensive to fix after the money has gone.

Buyers usually skip these steps because a website looks professional and the prices look good. Neither proves anything. The checks below are the ones that do.

## Step 1: Confirm the legal entity

Every legitimate distributor in the United States is registered as a business entity (a corporation, LLC or partnership) in at least one state. Ask for the exact legal name and the state where it's registered. Then look it up yourself.

Each state's secretary of state or business registration office offers a search. In New Jersey, for example, the Division of Revenue & Enterprise Services runs a Business Records Service where you can search by business name, principal or entity ID and see a business's status. Browsing is free.

What to look for:

- **Name match.** The registered name should match the name on the quote, the invoice and the bank account. A trade name ("doing business as") is fine, but the legal entity behind it should be clear.
- **Status.** Active or in good standing. A revoked or dissolved entity is a stop sign.
- **Formation date.** A company formed a few weeks ago isn't automatically a problem, but it doesn't match a pitch about decades of experience.
- **Registered address.** Compare it with the address on the website.

> **Note:** A registered agent address is often a law firm or a filing service, not the company's office. That's normal. What matters is that the business has a physical place of operation it will tell you about.

## Step 2: Check the address and phone number

Search the business address on a map and look at the street view. You're not looking for a grand building. You're looking for something consistent with what the distributor tells you: an office, a warehouse, a suite in a commercial building. A residential apartment or a mailbox store listed as a "distribution center" doesn't add up.

Call the main number listed on the website, not only the mobile number of the salesperson who emailed you. Ask for your contact by name. A real business can connect you, or at least confirm the person works there.

## Step 3: Look for an independent footprint

A business that has been trading for a while leaves traces that it doesn't control.

- **Business credit and directory listings.** Dun & Bradstreet assigns D-U-N-S numbers to businesses, and its lookup tool lets you search for a company's listing. A listing isn't a seal of approval, but a missing one for a supposedly established distributor is worth asking about.
- **Complaint and review searches.** The Federal Trade Commission suggests searching a company's name with words like "scam" or "complaint" before doing business with it. Read what comes up with some judgment: one angry review is noise, but a pattern of undelivered orders isn't.
- **Trade references.** Ask for two or three current customers or suppliers you can contact. Some distributors won't share customer names for confidentiality reasons, and that's fair, but they should be able to offer a bank or supplier reference instead.

## Step 4: Get tax and payment details in writing

Ask for a completed IRS Form W-9. It's the standard form businesses use to provide their taxpayer identification number, usually an Employer Identification Number (EIN), to the companies they deal with. The name on the W-9 should match the registered entity from Step 1.

Then get the payment instructions in writing, on company letterhead or in the distributor's account paperwork, and confirm them by phone using a number you found independently. The FBI warns that business email compromise often works exactly this way: a message that appears to come from a known vendor, asking for payment to a new account.

Rules to hold to:

- The receiving bank account should be in the business's legal name.
- Any change to payment details gets confirmed by phone, every time, using a number you already had.
- Walk away from anyone who insists on gift cards, cryptocurrency or wire transfers to an individual. The FTC flags these payment demands as common signs of a scam.

## Step 5: Ask how the goods are sourced

This step is about product quality and your own sales channel, not just fraud. Ask where the distributor buys the brands you're ordering: directly from the brand, from the brand's authorized distributors, or from other sources such as closeouts and the secondary market. All of these can be legitimate, but they carry different documentation and, for some brands, different marketplace rules.

If a distributor says it's authorized for a brand, you can ask for evidence or contact the brand directly. Some brands list their authorized distributors on their websites. A distributor that won't discuss sourcing at all, or claims authorization for everything it sells, deserves more scrutiny.

This matters most in categories with high counterfeit and warranty risk. In [electronics](/categories/electronics/), ask about serial numbers, model versions and warranty coverage for resold units. In [toys & games](/categories/toys-games/), ask whether the products are U.S. retail versions with the safety labeling and packaging the U.S. market requires.

## Step 6: Review a sample invoice

Ask for a redacted invoice from a recent order. If you sell on a marketplace, this is the document you may be asked to produce during an authenticity or brand review, so check it before you're relying on it.

A usable invoice shows:

- The distributor's legal name, address and phone number
- Your business name and address
- An invoice number and date
- Item descriptions with UPCs, model numbers or item numbers
- Quantities that match what you ordered

Invoices that look hand-edited, lack a supplier address or list items only as "assorted merchandise" won't help you when you need them.

## Step 7: Place a test order

Even a distributor that passes every check above can have operational problems. Start with an order small enough to absorb a problem and representative enough to learn from. When it arrives, compare cases and units against the packing slip and invoice, check dates and lot codes where relevant, and note how any shortage or damage is resolved.

## The verification checklist

| Check | How to do it | Pass | Stop and ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal entity | State business registry search | Active, name matches invoices | Dissolved, revoked or no record |
| Address | Map and street view | Commercial office or warehouse | Residential unit or mailbox store |
| Phone | Call the main listed number | Confirms your contact | No answer or number unrelated |
| Independent footprint | D-U-N-S lookup, complaint search | Consistent history | Pattern of non-delivery complaints |
| Tax ID | Form W-9 | Name matches registration | Refuses or name mismatch |
| Payment | Written instructions, confirmed by phone | Account in business name | Gift cards, crypto, personal account |
| Sourcing | Direct questions, brand confirmation | Clear, specific answers | Vague or blanket "authorized" claims |
| Invoice | Redacted sample | Full details and identifiers | Missing address or item detail |
| Test order | Small first order | Accurate, handled well | Short-shipped with no resolution |

> **In practice:** Legitimate distributors expect this. They verify their buyers too, with resale certificates, business registrations and sometimes trade references. If a supplier treats your questions as an insult, that tells you something.

## The questions go both ways

A careful distributor will ask you for a sales tax registration and resale certificate before selling tax-free.
