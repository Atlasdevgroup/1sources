To choose a wholesale distributor, start with your own buying pattern: your categories, your sales channel, how much you order and how often. Then compare distributors on five things that decide whether the relationship works: where their inventory comes from, how deep they carry your categories, what their minimums and case packs look like, how they price and bill, and whether their documentation will hold up if a marketplace, auditor or customer asks questions. Price per unit matters, but it's rarely the deciding factor once you have run a few orders.

Most buyers who switch distributors don't leave over a few cents a case. They leave because orders ship short, invoices don't match the packing slip, or nobody answers the phone when a pallet goes missing. The criteria below are built around avoiding that.

## Start with your own requirements

Before you look at a single price list, write down what you need. It takes ten minutes and saves weeks of comparing suppliers who were never a fit.

- **Categories and brands.** Which categories make up most of your revenue? Which specific brands do your customers ask for by name?
- **Channel.** A convenience store, a regional chain, an Amazon or Walmart Marketplace seller and a smaller wholesaler all need different things. Marketplace sellers, for example, usually need invoices that stand up to a brand or authenticity review.
- **Order size and frequency.** Are you buying a few mixed cases every week, or a few pallets once a month? This decides which minimums you can live with.
- **Where the goods go.** Your store, your own warehouse, a 3PL or a prep center. Each has different receiving rules.
- **Cash position.** Can you prepay every order for now, or do you need terms to make the math work?

Keep this list next to you on every call. When a distributor's answers don't match it, move on.

## The criteria that actually matter

### Where the inventory comes from

Ask directly: do you buy from the brand, from the brand's authorized distributors, or from other sources? Every answer can be legitimate, but you need to know which one you're getting. A distributor that buys from the brand or its authorized network can usually answer questions about lot codes, packaging changes and recalls. Goods bought on the secondary market can be genuine and perfectly legal to resell, but some brands and marketplaces treat them differently, so the paperwork matters more.

A good distributor answers this plainly. It won't claim to be "authorized" for brands it isn't, and it will tell you when a particular line is only available through a different channel.

### Depth in your categories

A catalog with thousands of SKUs is not useful if it's thin where you sell. Look at the categories that drive your revenue. If you run a [health & beauty](/categories/health-beauty/) aisle, check whether the distributor carries the core sizes and the variants that sell, not just a few hero items. The same goes for household basics, where fill rate on everyday items matters more than novelty.

### Minimums, case packs and mixing

Find out three numbers: the minimum order value, the minimum per SKU, and the case pack for the items you buy most. A low dollar minimum is less helpful than it sounds if every SKU ships in 48-unit cases and you only turn 6 a week. Ask whether you can mix brands on one order and whether inner packs are ever available.

### Pricing you can model

You should be able to build a landed cost for each SKU: unit cost, plus freight, plus any prep or handling you pay for, divided by the units you'll actually sell. Ask how prices are communicated (price list, offer sheet, quote per order), how often they change, and whether freight is quoted before you commit. A distributor whose prices are only available "on request" for every single order will be hard to plan around.

### Payment terms and billing

New accounts almost always start on prepayment. That's normal. What you want to know is whether terms are ever available after a trading history, and what the invoice will look like. Ask for a sample invoice with pricing redacted. It should show the distributor's legal name and address, your business name, item descriptions with UPCs or item numbers, quantities, and dates.

### Lead times, shipping and receiving

Ask how long orders take from confirmation to ship, how shortages are handled (backorder, substitution, or credit), and how you'll get tracking or a bill of lading. If you ship to a 3PL or prep center, confirm the distributor can follow that facility's labeling and appointment requirements.

### Who you talk to

One named contact who knows your account is worth more than a large sales team you can't reach. Pay attention to how long it takes to get answers during the evaluation. It rarely gets faster after the first order.

## A comparison you can use on every call

| What to check | What to ask | Good sign | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sourcing | "Where do you buy this brand?" | A direct, specific answer | "Don't worry about it" or a vague claim of authorization |
| Category depth | "What do you carry in my top category?" | Core sizes and variants in stock | Only closeouts or a handful of items |
| Minimums | "Order minimum, per-SKU minimum, case pack?" | Clear numbers in writing | Numbers that change each call |
| Pricing | "How do I get current pricing and freight?" | Price list or offer sheet, freight quoted up front | Price only after you've paid |
| Payment | "What methods do you accept?" | Business bank transfer, card, or check to a verified account | Gift cards, crypto, or a new bank account by email |
| Paperwork | "Can I see a sample invoice?" | Legal name, address, UPCs, quantities | Handwritten or editable-looking invoices |
| Shortages | "What happens if you ship short?" | A stated process: credit, backorder or refund | "It doesn't happen" |
| Contact | "Who handles my account?" | A named person with a direct line | A shared inbox only |

## Red flags that should end the conversation

Some warning signs are worth taking seriously on their own.

- **Unusual payment methods.** The Federal Trade Commission warns businesses that scammers often ask to be paid by wire transfer, cryptocurrency or gift cards. A real distributor has no reason to insist on the last two.
- **Changed bank details by email.** A request to send your payment to a "new" account is a classic sign of business email compromise, a scheme the FBI warns businesses about specifically. Always confirm by calling a number you already have on file, not one in the email.
- **Pressure to decide today.** Deals that expire within hours, or a price that only holds if you wire money now, are designed to stop you from checking.
- **Prices far below everyone else's.** Occasional closeouts are real. A full catalog of current, in-demand brands well below the market usually isn't.
- **No verifiable business.** If you can't match the company name to a state business registration, a physical address and a working phone number, stop. Tools like the Dun & Bradstreet D-U-N-S lookup can help confirm a company exists as described.

> **In practice:** A slow, careful distributor is a minor inconvenience. A fast, careless one can cost you a marketplace account or a pallet of unsellable product. When in doubt, favor the one that answers questions in writing.

## Know what kind of supplier you're talking to

"Distributor," "wholesaler" and "jobber" get used loosely, and the differences affect pricing, minimums and paperwork. A broker who never takes ownership of the goods is a different relationship from a distributor that holds inventory and invoices you directly. If the terms blur together, [Wholesale Distributor vs. Wholesaler vs. Jobber](/insights/wholesale-distributor-vs-wholesaler-vs-jobber/) explains how each one works.

## Have your own paperwork ready

Distributors are evaluating you too. Most will ask for your business name, address, tax ID and a resale certificate before quoting or shipping tax-free. Having these ready shortens approval and signals that you're a professional buyer. If you're not sure which certificate to send, read [How Resale Certificates Work for Wholesale Buyers](/insights/how-resale-certificates-work/).

## Run a test order before you commit

Even after good answers on every call, the first order is the real evaluation. Keep it small enough that a problem won't hurt, and large enough to be representative: a handful of your steady sellers across two or three brands.

When it arrives, check:

1. Case counts and units against the packing slip and the invoice.
2. Expiration or lot dates on anything that has them, especially health & beauty and food.
3. Packaging condition, and whether the product matches the current retail version.
4. Whether the invoice has everything you'd need to show a marketplace or auditor.
5. How any shortage or damage claim was handled, and how quickly.

If the distributor handles a problem on the first order well, that tells you more than a clean shipment would.
