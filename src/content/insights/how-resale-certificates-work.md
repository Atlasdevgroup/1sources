A resale certificate is a document a registered business gives its supplier to show that it's buying goods to resell, not to use. When the supplier accepts it in good faith, it doesn't charge sales tax on that purchase, and the tax is collected later, from the end customer, when the goods are sold at retail. In the United States, sales tax is set and administered by each state, so the certificate you use, and whether a given supplier can accept it, depends on the states involved.

That's the whole idea. The details are where buyers get caught out: an unregistered business trying to use a certificate, an out-of-state form a supplier can't accept, or a blanket certificate that went stale after the buyer moved. This guide covers how the system works for wholesale buyers and what to have ready before your first order.

> **Note:** This article is general information, not tax or legal advice. Rules change and differ by state. Confirm your obligations with your state's tax agency or a qualified tax professional.

## Why resale certificates exist

Sales tax is meant to be paid once, by the final consumer. If every business in a supply chain paid sales tax on its purchases, the same product could be taxed several times before it reached a shelf. The resale exemption prevents that. A distributor sells to a retailer tax-free under a resale certificate; the retailer collects tax from the shopper and remits it to the state.

The certificate is the paper trail that justifies the untaxed sale. If a state auditor later asks the supplier why it didn't charge tax on an invoice, the certificate on file is the answer.

## Key terms

- **Resale certificate:** A state document a reseller gives a supplier to buy goods for resale without paying sales tax. Some states call it a resale exemption certificate or a certificate of resale.
- **Sales tax permit / seller's permit / certificate of authority:** The state registration that lets you collect sales tax. New Jersey calls it a Certificate of Authority. You generally need this registration before you can issue a resale certificate.
- **Single-purchase certificate:** Covers one specific purchase.
- **Blanket certificate:** Covers ongoing purchases from the same supplier until it's revoked or expires.
- **Good faith:** The standard a supplier must meet when accepting a certificate. In broad terms, the certificate is complete, and the supplier has no reason to believe the information is false or that the goods aren't for resale.

## Who can use a resale certificate

In general, you can issue a resale certificate if you:

1. Are registered to collect sales tax in the relevant state (or, in some cases, hold a registration in your home state).
2. Are buying goods you intend to resell in the regular course of business, either as-is or as a component of something you sell.

You generally can't use one for things your business consumes, like shelving, cleaning supplies for your store, packaging you don't pass on, or products you take for personal use. If you buy inventory tax-free and later use some of it yourself, most states expect you to report and pay use tax on those items.

A handful of states, including Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon, have no statewide sales tax, and Alaska allows local sales taxes only. Businesses based there still deal with resale documentation when they buy from suppliers in states that do tax sales, which is where multistate certificates come in.

## How the process works, step by step

1. **Register with your state tax agency.** Apply for a sales tax permit in the state where you operate. The Federation of Tax Administrators keeps links to every state's tax agency.
2. **Get the right form.** Download the resale certificate your state issues, or a multistate form if your supplier accepts it.
3. **Complete it fully.** Business name, address, your state registration number, a description of what you'll resell, a signature and a date. Missing fields are the most common reason suppliers reject certificates.
4. **Send it to your supplier, not the state.** Resale certificates stay between buyer and seller. New Jersey's ST-3, for example, is marked "do not mail this form to the Division of Taxation."
5. **Keep a copy and keep it current.** Update the certificate when your business name, address or registration changes, and when a state's form is revised.

## Multistate certificates

If you buy from suppliers in several states, filling out a different form for each gets old quickly. Two multistate forms exist to help.

### Streamlined Sales Tax Certificate of Exemption

The Streamlined Sales and Use Tax Agreement is a cooperative effort among a group of member states to simplify sales tax administration. Its Certificate of Exemption (form F0003) is accepted by all Streamlined member states, and New Jersey is one of them. The form can be used for resale and for other exemptions, and it can be kept on paper or electronically. The purchaser is responsible for confirming it's eligible for the exemption it claims, and it's liable for tax, interest and possible penalties if it isn't.

### MTC Uniform Sales & Use Tax Resale Certificate

The Multistate Tax Commission publishes the Uniform Sales & Use Tax Resale Certificate – Multijurisdiction. According to the MTC, about three dozen states have indicated that it can be used as a resale certificate, but several attach conditions, and the certificate's own instructions list them state by state. Read those notes for every state you'll use it in.

### How the options compare

| Certificate | State-specific form (e.g. NJ ST-3) | Streamlined Certificate of Exemption | MTC Uniform Resale Certificate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Issued by** | One state's tax agency | Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board | Multistate Tax Commission |
| **Where it's accepted** | That state | Streamlined member states | States that have indicated acceptance, some with conditions |
| **Covers** | Resale (other exemptions use other forms) | Resale and several other exemption types | Resale |
| **Best for** | Buying mainly within one state | Buyers and sellers in member states | Buyers purchasing across many states |
| **Watch for** | Revised versions of the form | Not every exemption is valid in every member state | State-specific notes and restrictions |

> **In practice:** A supplier may still ask for its own state's form even if a multistate certificate is technically accepted there. That's a reasonable request, because the supplier is the one carrying the audit risk. Keep your state form and a multistate form ready so you can send whichever it asks for.

## New Jersey: Form ST-3

New Jersey uses Form ST-3, the Resale Certificate. A few features worth knowing:

- The purchaser must hold a valid New Jersey Certificate of Authority to collect sales tax.
- It can be issued as a single-purchase or a blanket certificate.
- It asks for your New Jersey taxpayer identification number, business name and address, and a description of the business and goods.
- The seller keeps it, and the form says it must be retained for four years from the date of the last sale it covers.

New Jersey's Tax Topic Bulletin S&U-6 explains the good-faith standard in more detail. Among other things, it says a seller is relieved of liability when a fully completed certificate is received within 90 days of the sale, and that for a purchaser not registered in New Jersey, the certificate can carry the purchaser's federal EIN or out-of-state registration number. The state also publishes Form ST-3NR for certain out-of-state purchasers who aren't registered in New Jersey, and it accepts the Streamlined Certificate of Exemption.

## When a state won't accept another state's certificate

This trips up a lot of online sellers. Some states accept resale certificates issued under another state's registration. Others require the buyer to register in that state, or to use that state's own form, before a supplier there can sell tax-free. If you're shipping goods into a state where you're not registered, check both the supplier's state rules and the delivery state's rules.

If a supplier can't accept your certificate, it has to charge sales tax. That isn't the supplier being difficult; it's the supplier following the law in its state. You may be able to recover the tax later through a credit or refund process, but that's slower and more work than getting the certificate right up front.

## What suppliers keep on file, and why they ask

From the supplier's side, every tax-free sale needs a valid certificate behind it. That's why a legitimate distributor will:

- Ask for a certificate before it quotes or ships tax-free, not after.
- Check that the certificate is complete, signed and dated.
- Reject a certificate that's clearly for a different business or a different state's rules.
- Ask for updates when forms are revised or your details change.

A supplier that never asks for a resale certificate, yet sells to you without tax, is either charging tax somewhere you haven't noticed or is taking a compliance risk that could come back to both of you. ## Common mistakes to avoid

- Using a resale certificate before your sales tax registration is active.
- Leaving the "description of goods" line blank or vague.
- Using a multistate form in a state that has restrictions on it, without reading the notes.
- Buying items for store use on the same tax-free invoice as your inventory.
- Forgetting to send an updated certificate after moving or changing entity type.

## Your next step

Before you apply with any new supplier, gather your state sales tax registration, a completed resale certificate for your home state, and a multistate form if you buy across state lines.
